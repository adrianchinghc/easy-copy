# Reference patterns: promise, work, proof

Use for complex offers, service pages, reference-site reviews and drafts that sound clear but still leave readers asking how it works. These extend the existing formulas rather than replace them.

## What was already covered

Outcome-first headlines, named jobs, short active verbs, repeated CTAs, real output examples, setup effort, timelines, adjacent proof, honest limits, pricing details and case studies already live in the other references. Do not call them new discoveries.

## What the live pages add

Observed on 6 October 2026. These are copy and information-design observations, not verified product capabilities or conversion results. Recheck the pages for later audits. Do not copy their claims into another brand.

- [Squad](https://squad.so/): a labelled demo follows work through visible states and pauses for a human decision. Permission and activity examples make control concrete. This extends output examples into a request-to-result story and turns broad trust language into a visible handoff.
- [Outrank](https://www.outrank.so/): the page connects a desired result to named deliverables and a staged process. It also shows editing, preview and cadence choices beside ongoing automation. This suggests separating the provider's work from downstream results and explaining the reader's role within that work.

The pages also contain long sentences, jargon, broad promises and competing wording. They are selective references, not authorities that override this skill's reading-level or honesty rules. We have not measured their sales or run their experiments.

## 1. Tie the promise to the work that could produce it

Before drafting, map four things for each main promise:

- **Deliverable:** what the buyer actually receives
- **Mechanism:** the concrete steps that produce it
- **Reader's part:** what they provide, check, decide or do next
- **Evidence:** what supports this exact claim

Keep the desired result distinct from the deliverable. Publishing an article is not proof that sales rose. Giving advice is not proof the client acted on it. Use a qualified result when the offer can only help. Preserve the same qualification throughout the page.

Original illustration for a service, not a client claim:

- Deliverable: a short list of late bills and next steps
- Mechanism: the team checks the records the client shares
- Reader's part: the client checks the list and chooses who to call
- Evidence: a labelled sample list; no promised cash recovered

Possible copy: "We check the bills you share. You get a list of who to call. You choose what to do next."

## 2. Show one complete case, including the handoff

Extend the existing examples bank into a single traceable story:

`Request or input → work done → output → reader's next step`

Choose one recognisable case. Reuse its names, amounts and dates. Show where the product stops or asks for a decision. For services, show what the team prepares and what the client decides. Use a screenshot, sample document or short text walkthrough only when it genuinely clarifies the work.

Original demo illustration: a customer asks about a late order; the tool checks its status; a reply draft appears; the shop owner reviews it. Do not silently turn a draft into a sent reply.

Label invented demo data where it appears. State if the example is a mock-up, a real sample or an interactive demo. Never describe a static screenshot as usable. Keep the main explanation understandable without images.

## 3. Make control part of the explanation

State the actual choices alongside the relevant action: review, edit, approve, change timing, stop, or hand back to a person. Choose only the controls that exist.

Use `Who does what → when the reader can act → what happens next`.

Do not turn an optional review into a required approval, or vice versa. Do not add a permissions promise, automatic action, log or refund policy because the reference site has one. Avoid a universal claim if exceptions exist.

## 4. Use the right evidence for the promise

- A sample output supports a claim about what is delivered
- A walkthrough supports a claim about how the steps fit together
- A real control screen can support a claim about a user choice
- A customer result needs its source, period and relevant context
- A business outcome does not follow automatically from a count of outputs

Label the evidence's limits. A fictional demo is an illustration, not a testimonial. A testimonial is one person's report, not a typical-results guarantee. Observing a pattern on a successful-looking site does not prove it converts.

## 5. Adapt and check the whole page

Use the brand's own vocabulary and tone. For advisory or professional services, prefer people, decisions and deliverables over unattended-software language. Keep the offer's actual price, scope, responsibilities and uncertainty.

Read just the headings: do they tell a coherent story? Then check every repeated fact across the hero, process, demo, price, FAQ and CTA. Look especially for draft/send, optional/required, daily/monthly, included/extra and demo/live conflicts. Resolve contradictions from the source of truth; do not pick whichever version sounds stronger.

If a claim lacks evidence, narrow or omit it. Keep the grade-three target, meaning-preservation check and no-ai-slop pass. When proposing tests, name the uncertain choice and a measurable goal; do not promise a conversion uplift.

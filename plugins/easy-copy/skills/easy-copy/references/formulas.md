# Formulas

Fill in the brackets. Every formula has a worked example, taken from a real rewrite for Second Team, a tool for businesses with repeat customers. Swap in your own product.

## Page skeleton (in this order)

Each section has one job. Use as many sections as the page needs, but keep the order.

1. **Hero:** what you get, what it is, and the next step.
2. **Problem:** three named ways the pain happens, each with an example.
3. **Jobs:** what the product does, as named jobs.
4. **Output:** what lands on your desk, shown word for word (see "Show the output").
5. **How it works:** three or four steps in a three-part headline, plus how much effort setup takes.
6. **A day or first month with it:** a timeline (optional, but strong).
7. **Trust:** one-line promises, including what it won't do.
8. **Who it's for:** segments the reader recognises.
9. **Proof:** real results only. Skip this section until you have them.
10. **FAQ:** answers that open with Yes or No.
11. **Final CTA:** restate the outcome, then the action.

For pages other than the home page (one page per job, pricing, comparison, case study, About), use the templates in `references/page-types.md`.

## Hero

**Eyebrow (above the headline):** who it's for, by name.
→ "For distributors, agencies and contract services"

**Headline: outcome first, 10 words or fewer.** Pick one shape:
- `More [good thing]. Fewer [bad thing].` → "More repeat business. Fewer unpaid invoices."
- `Get your [who] [doing the outcome] again.` → "Get your regulars ordering again."
- `[Category] to [job].` → (Squad) "AI teammates to do your work"
- `[Verb] [outcome] [before something bad].` → "Renew more contracts before they end."

**Subhead: what it is and what it does. The product is the subject and every sentence is short.**
`[Product] [reads/takes] your [input] every [time]. It finds [what]. Then it [makes/writes] [output] for you. [Tiny action], and [result].`
→ "Second Team reads your invoices every week. It finds the customers to call. Then it writes the message for you. You tap yes, and it goes out."

**Example line (optional, one per audience segment, as full sentences):**
→ "A café stopped ordering. A client's deal ends next month. An invoice is 45 days late."

**Button and risk reducers:** "Join the waitlist" · "Free to join · No card needed"

## Problem

**H2:** `[Number] ways [who] lose [X] without noticing.` → "Three ways businesses lose money without noticing."

**Card:** `[Bad thing happens]. [Example A]. [Example B]. [Why nobody notices].`
→ "Orders get smaller. A hotel orders 40% less. A site cuts cleaning to once a week. They never left, so nobody sees it."

## Jobs (the main feature format)

Name each feature as a task you'd hand a new hire.

```
[Job name]                    ← 1–3 words people say: "Invoice chaser"
[When it runs]                ← optional: "Every Monday"
[Verb]s [object] and [verb]s [result].   ← one line, product as subject
For example: [case A]. [case B].         ← one per segment, full sentences
```
→ "**Invoice chaser** · Lists late invoices and writes a kind reminder for each one. For example: an invoice is 45 days late."

**H2:** `[Number] jobs it does for you, every [time].`

## How it works

**H2, a three-part headline.** Use it once per page and put each sentence on its own line.
`It [verb]s [X]. It [verb]s [Y]. It [verb]s [Z].` → "It reads your invoices. It finds who to call. It writes the message."

**Steps:** title each one by what the reader gets ("See who to call"), not by an internal name. If you must use a product name ("Monday Money List"), explain it in the same card.

## Trust

`Nothing [risky thing] without your [yes].` → "Nothing reaches a customer without your yes."
`We only [safe action]. We never [feared action].` → "We only read your invoices. We never change them."

## Who it's for

**H2:** `For [who], whose [customers] [do the thing] again and again.`
List segment names the reader recognises: "Packaging, Food service suppliers, Hardware…"

## FAQ

`[Yes/No]. [One line on why]. [One line on what you do].`
→ "No. There's nothing to set up. You share your invoices and say yes to the messages."

Write the questions from real objections: price, setup, fit, trust, timing.

## Final CTA

**H2:** restate the hero outcome as an action. → "Get more repeat business from customers you already have."
**Button:** the same as the hero button.

## Show the output (the strongest proof when you have no customers yet)

Don't just describe what the reader gets. Show it, word for word: the email, report, list or message the product makes. Label it as an example, and use realistic names and amounts.

```
What lands on your desk
From: Tessa at Brightlane
Subject: Invoice #1382, a friendly nudge
Hi Dana, invoice #1382 for $1,840 was due on Friday. I've attached it again…
```

## Timelines

**A day in the life.** Use clock times and short lines. Show what the product does and what the reader does.
→ "8:40 AM You ask it to chase unpaid invoices. · 10:20 AM Seven drafts wait for you. You approve them on the train."

**Your first month.** Show what happens and how much effort it takes, so the reader can picture getting started.
→ "Day 0: Connect your invoices. Five minutes. · Day 1: The first list shows 7 late invoices. · Week 1: The drafts need no edits. · Month 1: Chasing runs in the background."

## Effort and time to value

Say how long setup takes, in plain units. → "One click, and you're done." · "Five minutes." · "Ready on day one. No setup from you."

## What you get

Use 3–4 bullets. Each one is an outcome with a number or a time.
→ "Every late invoice gets a reminder within one business day." · "One polite voice on every reminder." · "A full log you can show your accountant."

## Feature line with its benefit

`[Feature], so [benefit].` → "Its own email address, so replies come back to it." · "Read-only access, so it can't change your books."

## Trust: say what it won't do

Fears shrink when you name the limit. `It [does X]. It doesn't [feared thing].`
→ "It reads invoices and payment status. It doesn't move money."

**Trust ramp:** let control loosen over time. → "At first, every message waits for your yes. Once the drafts look right, let it send the small ones and keep the big ones for you."

## Problem as a plain truth

Two short sentences the reader already believes. No drama and no metaphor.
→ "Unpaid invoices don't age well. The longer a reminder waits, the harder the talk gets."

## Menu and label text

In menus and feature lists, give each product name a short plain line. → "**Email inboxes**: its own address, so replies come back to it."

## Other formats

- **Email.** The subject names the outcome or a specific case ("Hilltop Roasters hasn't ordered in 51 days"). The first line says why you're writing. One idea, one ask, one link. Sign off as a person.
- **Ad.** Hook with a specific pain case. The next line says what the product does, with the product as subject. The CTA says what happens. No metaphors in the hook.
- **Social post.** Open with a concrete case or number. Write one short paragraph per idea. End with one question or one action.
- **Sales page or deck.** Use the page skeleton, with one section or slide per job.
- **In-app and UI text.** Put the verb first on buttons ("Approve and send"). Error messages say what happened and what to do next. Never blame the user.
- **Short form (bios, meta descriptions, taglines).** Say what it is, who it's for and the outcome, in one sentence of 160 characters or fewer.

## Examples bank (build it before writing)

1. **Make one example per audience segment** and reuse it across the page. → a café that stopped ordering (distributors), a deal ending (agencies), a cleaning contract about to end (contract services).
2. **Use realistic business names**, the kind you'd see on a real invoice: Hilltop Roasters, The Marlow Hotel, Butter & Rye Bakery. Never use "ABC Cafe", "Acme" or "Company X". Keep them made up and label them as examples.
3. **Keep numbers the same everywhere.** If an invoice is 45 days late in the hero, it's 45 days late on every page.
4. **Match the currency and region** in the project context.
